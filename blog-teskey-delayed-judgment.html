Backward-Engineered Justice: Reversing Convictions Based on Delayed Judgments and R. v. Teskey

When a trial concludes, an accused person is constitutionally entitled to know exactly why they are being convicted. In Canada, judicial reasons are the primary mechanism of public and legal accountability. They prove to the parties, the public, and the higher courts that the judge evaluated the evidence with an open, impartial mind before rendering a verdict.

But what happens when a judge stands up in court, announces a bare, one-sentence conviction, and then takes months—or even a year—to produce the actual written reasons explaining their choice?

Under Canadian law, this is not merely an administrative bottleneck. It directly threatens the core of trial fairness. When a judge splits a verdict from its legal rationale by an inordinate amount of time, it creates a dangerous risk that the written reasons are not an honest reflection of a neutral deliberation, but are instead an after-the-fact exercise in backward-engineering a pre-determined outcome.

If you or a loved one were convicted after an extended delay, your case may engage the landmark Supreme Court of Canada framework established in R. v. Teskey, [2007] 2 S.C.R. 267. Here is a look at how this critical ground of appeal works, and how it was successfully deployed to overturn an unjust verdict.

The Presumption of Integrity vs. "Result-Driven Reasoning"

Ordinarily, Canadian appellate courts grant trial judges a strong "presumption of integrity and impartiality". Higher courts presume that a trial judge's written reasons honestly reflect the internal cognitive process that led them to the verdict.

However, the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. Teskey established that this presumption is not absolute. It can be completely shattered if the timeline and context of the judgment give rise to a reasonable apprehension of result-driven reasoning. This occurs when an ordinary, informed observer looks at the record and walks away with the distinct impression that the judge chose a winner first, and then spent months tailoring and crafting the written text to defend a conclusion they had already committed to.

The Seven Teskey Warning Signs

To successfully argue a Teskey violation on appeal, the appellant must present cogent evidence to displace the presumption of judicial regularity. The Supreme Court identified seven specific warning signs that indicate a judge may have engaged in result-driven reasoning:

  • Obvious Deliberation Distress: The trial judge displays visible, ongoing difficulty in arriving at a verdict in the months following the close of evidence.
  • A Bare Declaration of Guilt: The judge delivers an absolutely bare oral declaration of guilt without providing any immediate indication of the underlying legal or factual reasoning.
  • Willingness to Reconsider: The trial judge expresses an explicit willingness to immediately reconsider the verdict right after announcing it.
  • Deep Evidentiary Complexity: The nature of the trial evidence is highly complex or circumstantial, fundamentally crying out for a detailed, contemporaneous analysis before any safe verdict can be reached.
  • Ignoring Counsel Requests: The trial judge repeatedly fails to respond to ongoing requests from counsel to produce the missing reasons.
  • Anachronistic Rationale: The eventual written reasons make explicit references to events that occurred long after the initial oral verdict was announced, strongly suggesting the rationale was constructed post-decision.
  • Inordinate, Unexplained Delay: An exceptional delay occurs in delivering the reasons, coupled with a complete absence of any explanation indicating the reasons were ready or intentionally deferred for a valid legal purpose.

A Real-World Victory: R. v. Jasmine Worrell

The structural damage caused by result-driven reasoning is vividly illustrated by the summary conviction appeal of R. v. Worrell, 2025 ONSC 6859, which was successfully argued by appellate lawyer Trevor Lau.

The Trial and the Timeline

In Worrell, the appellant faced allegations of assault and assault causing bodily harm stemming from a highly chaotic physical altercation in a downtown hotel suite. The evidence at trial was highly conflicting, relying almost entirely on competing witness credibility, and involved intricate details regarding a complex self-defence claim.

The timeline of the judgment was extraordinary:

  • August 31, 2023: Trial submissions are fully completed.
  • December 11, 2023: After missing multiple self-imposed deadlines, the trial judge delivers a bare oral "summary" conviction, promising full written reasons "early next week".
  • November 4, 2024: The actual written reasons are finally delivered to counsel—nearly 11 months after the oral conviction and over a year after submissions closed.

The Appellate Strategy

On appeal before the Superior Court of Justice, Trevor Lau systematically argued that the trial judge's lengthy delay, combined with a bare oral verdict, completely destroyed public and legal confidence in the integrity of the adjudication.

The bare oral summary provided no substantive analysis explaining how the court resolved major contradictions in the complainant's evidence, failing the structural requirements of a valid judgment.

When the written reasons finally surfaced nearly a year later, they bore the classic hallmarks of a Teskey violation. The judgment read as a defensive document, hyper-focused on discrediting the appellant's testimony while completely rejecting or omitting critical factual concessions that the Crown prosecutor had already explicitly accepted on the record during submissions. The written judgment also contained an incomplete sentence in an analysis on a key issue.

The reviewing judge, Mainville J., accepted Trevor's arguments. The court held that because the long-delayed written reasons created a reasonable apprehension that the judge had backward-engineered a justification for a conviction she committed to long ago, the reasons could not be safely considered on appeal. Left with only a bare, legally insufficient oral summary, the Court allowed the appeal, quashed the findings of guilt, and ordered a brand-new trial.

Turning a Delayed Judgment Into an Appellate Victory

Launching an appeal under the Teskey framework requires an incredibly specialized, exhaustive review of the trial records. An appellate advocate cannot simply point to a long wait time on its own; delay alone does not automatically equate to result-driven reasoning.

Instead, your lawyer must carefully audit the transcripts of trial submissions, compile the precise timeline of judicial promises and missed deadlines, and demonstrate that the final written reasons read as a one-sided, defensive justification rather than an impartial, contemporaneous analysis of the evidence. When executed properly, a Teskey application remains one of the most powerful safeguards available to dismantle an unfair conviction and restore the presumption of innocence.
