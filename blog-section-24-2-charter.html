The Evidentiary Kill-Switch: How Canadian Courts Exclude Evidence Under Section 24(2) of the Charter

In the public imagination, getting a criminal case "thrown out on a technicality" is viewed as a systemic loophole. You see it constantly in crime dramas: a police officer makes a procedural misstep, a defence lawyer gives a fiery speech, and a major drug or weapon case vanishes into thin air.

In the real world of Canadian constitutional litigation, there are no "technicalities." There is only the Supreme Court of Canada's strict legal architecture under Section 24(2) of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

Section 24(2) dictates that when evidence is obtained in a manner that violates your Charter rights, it must be completely excluded from the trial if its admission would bring the administration of justice into disrepute. It is the ultimate systemic quality-control mechanism—designed not to reward an accused person, but to keep the justice system above reproach.

If you are facing serious charges where police uncovered physical contraband or extracted a confession, you need to understand exactly how the Section 24(2) "kill-switch" operates.

1. The Threshold: "Obtained in a Manner"

Before a judge ever looks at the impact of a police mistake, the defence must clear a preliminary legal hurdle: proving that the evidence was actually "obtained in a manner" that infringed a Charter right.

Following the binding framework set out by the Court of Appeal for Ontario in R. v. Pino, Canadian courts reject a narrow, mechanical connection. Instead, judges must adopt a generous approach that reviews the entire "chain of events" between the citizen and the state. The required connection between the police's unconstitutional overstep and the discovery of the evidence can be established in three distinct ways:

  • Causal: The breach directly caused the discovery of the evidence.
  • Temporal: The breach and the discovery of the evidence occurred close together in time.
  • Contextual: The breach and the discovery are inextricably linked within the same course of police conduct or transaction.

If the state’s constitutional violations are temporally or contextually intertwined with the discovery of the evidence, Section 24(2) is fully engaged.

2. The Core Test: The Three Lines of Inquiry in R. v. Grant

Once a Charter breach is established, the judge must execute a strict balancing act. Under the foundational Supreme Court ruling of R. v. Grant, [2009] 2 S.C.R. 353, the court evaluates three specific lines of inquiry to determine whether admitting the unconstitutionally seized evidence would degrade public confidence in the rule of law:

Branch 1: The Seriousness of the Charter-Infringing State Conduct

This branch acts as a measurement of police culpability. Courts place state errors on a clear spectrum. At one end are innocent, inadvertent, or minor good-faith mistakes. At the more serious end is conduct displaying a wilful, reckless, or flagrant disregard for constitutional standards. If a breach is the product of a systemic or institutionalized police practice, the state misconduct is treated with severe gravity. Admitting evidence obtained through reckless state overreach signals to the public that the courts condone illegal law enforcement.

Branch 2: The Impact of the Breach on the Charter-Protected Interests of the Accused

This line of inquiry looks at how severely the police intrusion undermined your individual human dignity, autonomy, or privacy. While an individual carries a diminished expectation of privacy when traveling in a vehicle on a public highway, your privacy expectations peak regarding your bodily integrity, your home, or the highly personal biological data locked inside your digital communications. If the police execute a sweeping, ungrounded search that flagrantly strips away your core privacy interests, this branch pulls heavily toward total exclusion.

Branch 3: Society’s Interest in an Adjudication of the Case on its Merits

This branch addresses the truth-seeking function of a criminal trial. Judges must evaluate whether the unconstitutionally seized evidence is inherently reliable and essential to the prosecution's case. While society has a high interest in seeing serious offences adjudicated on their merits using objective, reliable physical evidence (like a loaded gun or high-purity narcotics), this interest must never blindly trump individual constitutional rights. If the first two branches establish a strong, joint case for exclusion, society's short-term clamour for a conviction will seldom tip the balance toward admissibility.

The Strategic Defensive Safeguard

Winning a Section 24(2) exclusion application requires a meticulous, line-by-line constitutional audit of the entire state interaction. Your defence advocate must systematically dissect the officer's initial notes, cross-examine the validity of their subjective grounds, trace the exact temporal sequence of the arrest, and construct an unassailable roadmap demonstrating how the state's errors compromised long-term systemic integrity. When executed with precision, Section 24(2) remains the most potent weapon available to protect individual liberty and preserve the rule of law.
