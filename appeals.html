Appeals

A criminal conviction or an overly harsh sentence does not have to be the end of the road. If you have been wrongfully convicted or received a disproportionate sentence, you have the right to challenge the trial judge's decision.

Grounds for a Criminal Appeal in Ontario

Appeals are highly complex, technical legal proceedings. They are not a "do-over" of your trial, but rather a targeted challenge to identify legal errors made by the trial judge or jury. Successful appeals generally fall into two categories:

  • Conviction Appeals: Challenging the guilty verdict based on errors of law (e.g., admitting evidence that violated your Charter rights, misapplying legal standards, or giving incorrect instructions to a jury), a misapprehension of the evidence, or an unreasonable verdict.
  • Sentence Appeals: Arguing that the punishment imposed by the trial judge was demonstrably unfit, excessive, or failed to properly consider mitigating factors.

The path of a criminal appeal follows a strict, highly regulated sequence:

  1. Filing the Notice of Appeal: Your lawyer drafts and serves the Crown with the formal Notice within 30 days of sentence, locking in your intent to appeal.
  2. Securing Bail Pending Appeal: If sentenced to jail, a separate release application is immediately argued so you can fight your case from home.
  3. Ordering Transcripts: Every word spoken during your trial is compiled into certified typed transcripts.
  4. Perfection of the Appeal: Your lawyer files the complete trial record, an Appeal Book, and a written argument called a Factum outlining the trial errors.
  5. The Oral Hearing: Lawyers present live arguments and answer intensive questioning before the appellate judge(s).
  6. The Decision: The court issues a formal judgment either dismissing the appeal, ordering a new trial, or entering a total acquittal.

Ordinarily, appeals are restricted strictly to the evidence heard at trial. However, you can introduce new evidence by filing a formal application for Fresh Evidence under the binding Palmer test. The court will only accept it if the evidence is highly credible, relevant, could not have been discovered before trial with reasonable diligence, and could realistically have changed the verdict.

You launch an appeal against a sentence by proving that the trial judge imposed a penalty that is demonstrably unfit or made an error in principle. This includes showing that the judge misapplied statutory guidelines, failed to consider mandatory mitigating factors, or overemphasized a specific aggravating element to create an unconscionable sentence.

Winning an appeal results in one of three primary judicial remedies:

  • A New Trial is Ordered: The appellate court strikes down the original conviction due to a material error but determines a trial must be re-run to achieve justice.
  • An Acquittal is Entered: The court completely overturns the conviction and determines the case cannot legally stand. You walk away with no criminal record and no further trials.
  • The Sentence is Altered: In a sentence-only appeal, the court reduces your jail time or modifies your probation conditions, allowing you to complete your penalty sooner.
