R. v. R.B.-C. (2026 SCC 30): Supreme Court Overrules Numerical Ceilings for Sentencing Delays

On September 11, 2026, the Supreme Court of Canada delivered a landmark judgment in R. v. R.B.-C. (2026 SCC 30), fundamentally reshaping how trial courts must evaluate post-verdict sentencing delay under Section 11(b) of the Charter. By explicitly overruling Ontario's five-month post-verdict ceiling established in R. v. Charley, the Court firmly shut the door on applying rigid Jordan numerical caps to the sentencing phase of a criminal prosecution.

The Death of the 5-Month Ceiling (R. v. Charley Overruled)

In R.B.-C., the Supreme Court held that the 18- and 30-month presumptive ceilings created in R. v. Jordan apply strictly to bringing an accused to trial, not to post-conviction proceedings. Writing for the unanimous Court, Justice Martin emphasized that sentencing is an inherently individualized, variable, and information-dependent process.

Unlike trial proceedings, sentencing frequently requires gathering vital information—such as Pre-Sentence Reports, psychiatric evaluations, Gladue reports, Impact of Race and Culture Assessments (IRCAs), or collateral immigration analyses—which cannot be neatly constrained by arbitrary, bright-line deadlines.

The "Markedly Longer" Standard: The New Test for Sentencing Delay

Instead of a fixed mathematical ceiling, the Court adopted the contextual "markedly longer" test. To establish a Section 11(b) breach post-conviction, an offender must prove that the time taken to impose sentence was markedly longer than it reasonably should have been in all the circumstances.

The assessment is objective and normative, evaluated against how a system ought to function in a free and democratic society. Key considerations include:

  • Overall Duration & Reasons: The total elapsed time balanced against specific explanations for delay.
  • Procedural Complexity: The need for disputed factual hearings (Gardiner hearings), victim impact statements, or complex legal submissions.
  • Case Management & Conduct: Whether the court and counsel managed issues efficiently rather than allowing them to unfold incrementally across multiple court dates.
  • Local Circumstances vs. Systemic Deficiencies: Local logistical realities remain relevant, but chronic under-funding or institutional inertia can never justify unreasonable delay.

Sentence Reduction Replaces the Default Stay of Proceedings

The judgment establishes a critical distinction regarding Section 24(1) Charter remedies. Before conviction, an unconstitutional delay taints the determination of guilt, making a stay of proceedings the mandatory default remedy.

Post-conviction, the presumption of innocence is extinguished. The offender's fair-trial interests are spent, and society's interest in imposing a fit, lawful sanction is heightened. Consequently:

  • Sentence Reduction is the Primary Remedy: A constitutional reduction in sentence answers the legal wrong by directly mitigating the harm caused by prolonged uncertainty or extended release conditions.
  • Stays of Proceedings Are Exceptional: A stay remains available for post-verdict delay only in the rarest, "clearest of cases" where no alternative remedy can preserve the integrity of the justice system.

Practical Takeaways for Criminal Defence Advocates

In R.B.-C. itself, the trial judge allowed collateral immigration inquiries to drag out across multiple court appearances without a clear timetable. Although the Court of Appeal erred by applying the five-month Charley ceiling, the Supreme Court affirmed that the 14-month post-verdict delay was markedly longer than necessary, upholding a four-month reduction of the offender's conditional sentence (reduced from 24 to 20 months).

For defence counsel, R.B.-C. signals a shift in strategy. While securing an absolute stay for post-verdict delay will now be extraordinary, establishing that a sentencing process dragged out inefficiently provides a potent Section 24(1) remedy to directly shave months off a client's custodial or conditional sentence.
